      About American Sign Language

      What is ASL?

      American Sign Language is a complete, natural language with its own grammar and syntax. It isn't a signed version of English. It's used primarily by Deaf communities across the US and parts of Canada.

      Its roots trace to 1817, when the American School for the Deaf opened in Hartford, Connecticut, founded by Thomas Gallaudet and Laurent Clerc, a Deaf teacher from France. ASL grew from French Sign Language blending with sign systems already used locally.

      Why it matters

      In the 1960s, linguist William Stokoe's research proved ASL has the full structure of a real language, a finding that reshaped how it's taught and recognized today.

      Learning it opens direct communication with the Deaf community, and shows up in fields like education, interpreting, and healthcare.

      Deaf awareness & etiquette

      Many in the Deaf community see themselves as a linguistic and cultural group, not as having something to "fix." A few habits go a long way:

      • Keep eye contact: it's how a signed conversation stays connected.
      • Get attention with a wave or a light tap on the shoulder, not shouting.
      • Face the person directly and keep your face visible; facial expression carries grammar, not just emotion.
      • It's fine to ask to write something down instead of guessing. Don't fake understanding.

      How a sign works

      Stokoe identified that every sign is built from a few parameters: handshape, location, movement, and palm orientation, plus facial expression, which changes meaning the way tone of voice does in speech.

      That's also why LinguaWave's camera tracks motion and position rather than grading a single still frame.
