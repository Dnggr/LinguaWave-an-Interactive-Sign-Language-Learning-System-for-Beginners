1 The Story of ASL

Long before ASL had a name, there was a place where it didn't need one. On Martha's Vineyard, starting in the 1700s, a hereditary form of deafness ran so deep through the island's population that much of the town — hearing and Deaf alike — grew up signing as a matter of course. Deaf islanders farmed, fished, argued town politics, and told jokes in sign alongside their hearing neighbors, in a community where being Deaf was closer to an ordinary trait than a barrier. Martha's Vineyard Sign Language never became a widely-known language in its own right, but it fed directly into what came next.

In 1817, Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet and Laurent Clerc founded the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut — the first permanent school for Deaf students in the United States. Clerc was Deaf himself, educated in France, and fluent in French Sign Language (LSF). At the school, LSF mixed with Martha's Vineyard Sign Language and the many home sign systems Deaf students already brought with them from across New England. Out of that mixing, over the generations that followed, American Sign Language took shape — a language born from a community, not designed by any single person.

Proving it was a language at all

For most of the 20th century, sign languages were widely dismissed — even by well-meaning educators — as gesture, or pantomime, or a simplified stand-in for "real" language. That changed in the 1960s, when linguist William Stokoe published research showing ASL has everything a spoken language has: its own phonology (handshape, location, and movement, functioning the way sounds do in speech), its own grammar, its own way of building complex meaning. It was the first rigorous linguistic case that ASL wasn't broken English — it was its own language, and the argument reshaped how linguists, educators, and eventually the public understood sign language everywhere, not only in the U.S.

A community that spoke up for itself

In March 1988, Gallaudet University — the world's only university built specifically for Deaf and hard-of-hearing students — announced a new president. Every finalist for the job was Deaf except the one the board picked. Students shut the campus down in protest for a week, and "Deaf President Now" became a rallying cry that reached national news. The board reversed course, and I. King Jordan became Gallaudet's first Deaf president. The protest is remembered as a turning point for Deaf pride and self-determination — proof that the Deaf community wasn't just the subject of decisions made about it, but a community capable of making its own.

Today, ASL is the primary language for a large community of Deaf and hard-of-hearing people across the United States and Canada — estimates commonly range from several hundred thousand to over a million users, though sign language use is notoriously hard to survey precisely. It's taught in thousands of schools and universities, recognized as a foreign language for academic credit in many U.S. states, and — like any living language — still changing.

2 A Few Things Worth Knowing

  • Deaf culture is a culture, not just an absence of hearing. Many Deaf people describe Deafness as an identity and community, not a deficit to be fixed.
  • ASL isn't signed English. Its grammar and word order are its own — a fluent ASL sentence doesn't map word-for-word onto an English one.
  • Facial expression is grammar, not just emotion. Eyebrow position and head tilt can be the difference between a statement and a question in ASL.
  • Learning ASL is a long-term skill, like any language — a little consistent practice beats an occasional long session, which is exactly what the path after this page is built around.
